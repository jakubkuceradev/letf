Do leveraged ETFs belong in a long-term portfolio?

We rebuilt daily 2x and 3x S&P 500 funds back to 1957 and simulated thousands of lump-sum portfolios. Leverage raises the typical outcome. It does not help the bad ones.

What a daily leveraged ETF does

A 3x S&P 500 fund aims to return three times the index's return every day, not over any longer period. To do that it holds three dollars of index exposure for every dollar invested, mostly through swaps, and pays to borrow the extra two dollars at an overnight rate plus a spread. It also charges an expense ratio of a little under 1% a year.

Resetting every day has a cost of its own. After a 10% fall, a 3x fund is down 30% and needs a 43% gain to recover, while the index needs only 11%. In choppy markets these losses compound, and over decades they can outweigh the extra return. Whether they do is an empirical question, which is why the funds' long-run behaviour has to be simulated rather than read off a formula.

What the model leaves out

The simulation only recombines the past. Its worst stretches come from 1973 to 1974, 1987, 2000 to 2002, and 2008, and it contains nothing as deep as 1929 to 1932. It breaks any pattern that lasts longer than a year, such as valuations drifting back over decades. With … of data, a 50-year path reuses much of the same history. It ignores taxes, trading costs, and the chance that a fund closes, and it models a single lump sum, not regular contributions.

So should you hold one?

A leveraged ETF is a bet on the middle and upper part of the distribution. If your priority is to avoid the worst outcomes, the simulation says to hold none: every step toward leverage lowers the bad cases. If you care about the typical outcome, the two funds differ. For 3x, median wealth peaks at about … of the portfolio and falls beyond it, because the daily-reset losses grow faster than the extra return. For 2x, median wealth keeps rising up to …, but the bad cases and the drawdowns get worse all the way. Either way the portfolio has to be rebalanced.
