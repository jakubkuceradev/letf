Do leveraged ETFs belong in a long-term portfolio?

We rebuilt daily 2x and 3x S&P 500 funds back to 1957 and simulated thousands of lump-sum portfolios. Over decades, a small slice of a 3x fund barely touches the bad outcomes and lifts the typical one. The price is a much rougher ride.

The short answer

For most investors, no. For a patient investor with a very long horizon who can sit through deep losses, a small slice may be worth it.

What a daily leveraged ETF does

A 3x S&P 500 fund aims to return three times the index's return every day, not over any longer period. To do that it holds three dollars of index exposure for every dollar invested, mostly through swaps, and pays to borrow the extra two dollars at an overnight rate plus a spread. It also charges an expense ratio of a little under 1% a year.

Resetting every day has a cost of its own. After a 10% fall, a 3x fund is down 30% and needs a 43% gain to recover, while the index needs only 11%. In choppy markets these losses compound, and over decades they can outweigh the extra return. Whether they do is an empirical question, which is why the funds' long-run behaviour has to be simulated rather than read off a formula.

The ride, and the person holding it

The numbers above assume an investor who holds on and rebalances every year without fail. That is the hard part. An investor who sells after such a loss, or stops rebalancing, locks in the damage and misses the recovery that makes the median look good. A leveraged fund is a speculative asset inside a long-term portfolio, and it only pays if it is treated like one: small, rebalanced mechanically, and left alone.

What the model leaves out

The simulation only recombines the past. Its worst stretches come from 1973 to 1974, 1987, 2000 to 2002, and 2008, and it contains nothing as deep as 1929 to 1932. It breaks any pattern that lasts longer than a year, such as valuations drifting back over decades. With about … years of data, a 50-year path reuses much of the same history, so the longest horizons are the least certain. It ignores taxes, trading costs, and the chance that a fund closes, and it models a single lump sum, not regular contributions.

So should you hold one?

If the bad outcomes are what you care about, hold none: at the 10th percentile the best share in a leveraged fund is zero at … of … horizons for 3x. If you have decades ahead, can stomach losing most of a slice in a bad year, and will keep rebalancing through it, a small slice of a 3x fund has historically cost little in the bad cases and added meaningfully to the typical outcome. That is a narrow group of investors. For everyone else, the plain index fund is the better trade, not because leverage loses on average, but because the ride makes it easy to lose by quitting.
