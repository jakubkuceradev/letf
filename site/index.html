Do leveraged ETFs belong in a long-term portfolio?

We modelled daily 2x and 3x S&P 500 funds, checked the model against the real funds, rebuilt their returns back to 1957, and simulated thousands of 50-year futures. A little leverage can suit some long-term investors. Whether an LETF is the best way to get it is a separate question.

Leveraged ETFs come with a warning label: they are built for short-term trading, and holding them for long periods can produce returns very different from their stated multiple. Yet long backtests of a fund like UPRO, the 3x S&P 500 ETF, look spectacular. Both statements are true, which makes the question worth answering carefully: what happens if a long-term investor holds part of a portfolio in one for decades?

The real funds are too young to answer that. The oldest daily S&P 500 leveraged fund still trading dates from 1993. So most of this article is about method: how the funds were modelled, how well the model matches them, what their returns would have looked like since 1957, what their leverage costs, and how the simulation turns one history into many possible futures. The results and a short answer are at the end.

What a daily leveraged ETF does

A 3x S&P 500 fund aims to return three times the index's return every day, not over any longer period. To do that it holds three dollars of index exposure for every dollar invested, mostly through swaps with banks, and pays to borrow the extra two dollars at an overnight rate plus a spread. It also charges an expense ratio of a little under 1% a year.

Resetting every day has a cost of its own. After a 10% fall, a 3x fund is down 30% and needs a 43% gain to recover, while the index needs only 11%. In choppy markets these losses compound, and over decades they can outweigh the extra return. How much they do depends on the path the market takes, which is why the funds' long-run behaviour has to be simulated rather than read off a formula.

Modelling what the funds cost

The model describes a fund's daily return as its leverage times the S&P 500's total return, minus three costs: financing on the borrowed part, the expense ratio, and everything else, such as the spread banks charge on the swaps and trading costs inside the fund. The expense ratio is known. We took each fund's actual yearly ratio from its annual reports filed with the SEC. That leaves two numbers to estimate per fund: how much of the federal funds rate it pays per dollar of assets, and a fixed spread.

What LETF leverage costs

The fitted costs also say what an LETF charges for its leverage. Per borrowed dollar, UPRO costs … times the federal funds rate plus … a year, and SSO … times the rate plus …, counting the expense ratio and the spread. At the federal funds rate of … on …, that is about … a year for 3x and … for 2x.

That puts LETFs in the middle. Index futures and SPX box spreads have recently cost about 0.3 to 0.8 points over the short-term rate, and the cheapest broker margin about 0.5 to 1.5 points, so an LETF costs roughly half a point to a point more than the cheapest routes. Margin at large retail brokers, at around 10% to 12% for smaller balances, costs about twice as much as an LETF.

Price is not the only difference. An LETF cannot lose more than the money in it and never triggers a margin call, it can be held in retirement accounts where margin is not allowed, and it does the rebalancing itself. Futures need large contract sizes and quarterly rolls, box spreads need options approval and careful execution, and margin loans can be called in a crash at the worst moment. The LETF's daily reset is also its weakness: it rebalances whether you want it to or not. For a small investor who wants a little leverage, an LETF is a reasonable route, but not a free one, and not the only one.

What the model leaves out

The simulation only recombines the past. Its worst stretches come from 1973 to 1974, 1987, 2000 to 2002, and 2008, and it contains nothing as deep as 1929 to 1932. It breaks any pattern that lasts longer than a year, such as valuations drifting back over decades. With about … years of data, a 50-year path reuses much of the same history, so the longest horizons are the least certain. The fitted borrowing spread is an average from 1993 onward and was probably wider in the high-rate 1970s. The model ignores taxes, trading costs, and the chance that a fund closes, and it covers a single lump sum, not regular contributions.

Conclusion

The results argue for a little leverage, not for leveraged ETFs as such. Over long horizons, a portfolio with slightly more than 1x exposure has historically given up little at the 10th percentile and gained meaningfully at the median: for 10% in a 3x fund over 50 years, … at the 10th percentile and … at the median. Below about the 10th percentile, and at shorter horizons, the plain index fund wins.

An LETF is one way to get that leverage. It costs about … a year per borrowed dollar today, more than futures, box spreads, or the cheapest margin, but much less than margin at large retail brokers, and it can never lose more than the money in it. The bigger cost is the ride: deeper drawdowns, longer recoveries, and a slice that can lose most of its value in a single bad year. For patient investors with decades ahead who will keep rebalancing through a crash, a small slice can be reasonable. For most investors, the index fund on its own remains the better choice, not because leverage loses on average, but because the ride makes it easy to lose by quitting.
